Two doors in

Why
two offers?

You close your eyes for thirty seconds. Your head makes the shopping list, replays the last meeting, wonders whether the gas is off. That's expected.

That's also what's frightening: you think there's something to get right, and you've never seen what it involves. We don't all start in the same place, so there are two doors.

The workshop is for seeing it up close, with nothing to believe. It doesn't turn you into a meditator. It lets you decide whether you want to go further.

The cycle is for learning the techniques for real. I pass them on, I help when you get stuck, I don't hold your hand. The work is yours to do.

A dirty window isn't cleaned in two minutes, or just once. You wipe it, the dust comes back, you wipe it again. Meditating is that same gesture, applied to attention: sit, notice it has wandered off, come back. Nothing to get right.

The individual cycle

The individual cycle means learning to meditate with me, one person at a time. Meditating means sitting quietly and training yourself to bring your attention back while thoughts pass. It's learned like a physical skill: with someone who shows you. I prescribe a set of techniques chosen for you, and I show them to you one by one, in Ath or online, on screen. If something sticks, we look at it together. The techniques are yours to practise: I can't do it for you. We start with a short time, and the practice asks you to come back to it every day. That's why I only open it to people who are determined to commit. €250, payable in one or two instalments.

Why techniques

The mind needs
a support.

Try it now: notice where your attention goes when you don't direct it. It leaves. You observe it; you don't grade it.

The techniques are what you come back to when it has gone. Without a support, you fight your thoughts, or follow them to the end. With a support, you notice, you come back, you start again. It isn't glamorous. It's a handrail: you don't look at it, but you're glad to have it on the stairs.

In the Yoga Sūtras (I.12), Patañjali describes two stages: repeated practice (abhyāsa), then detachment (vairāgya). The support first, the detachment afterwards.

According to The Path of Unfoldment, badly matched techniques have no effect, or an effect that doesn't suit. That's why the cycle is one-to-one: we look at your starting point, I prescribe the set that fits you, we adjust.

An example from my own life. In the car, someone makes a manoeuvre that annoys me. The anger rises anyway, the body reacts before I do. But sometimes I notice it, return to pranayama (breathing at a prescribed rhythm, one of my techniques) and let go faster. Not every time.

The tradition describes supports that matter less over the years. I don't know whether that happens for everyone, or when.

The method in three lines

What it is,
what it isn't.

One method, one lineage

IFSU Vedic meditation follows the Path of Unfoldment method, taught by Gururaj Ananda Yogi. IFSU was founded in 1975: it's a non-profit association, present in 8 countries.

Nothing to believe

IFSU is non-sectarian, non-dogmatic, compatible with all beliefs — or none. You practise and you observe.

Regular work

It isn't five minutes on an app. Practice begins at 20 minutes a day, then builds up gradually.

My name is Marjorie. I've practised IFSU Vedic meditation since 2013, passed on by Breeyana. Co-lead of IFSU French-speaking Belgium, I'm also on the international BFMS council (Belgium-France).

My role

I pass on the techniques as I received them, within a framework. I answer your questions and follow you through the cycle: when you get stuck, you tell me and we look together. What you find in them, I have no access to, and I don't try to guess.

“A true guide does not shine the light on you, but on the path, so that you do not stumble.” Gururaj Ananda Yogi

What I notice in my practice

Meditation made me look closely at the mind: how it spins, what it repeats. Part of what I call “me” comes from outside: habits, inheritances, other people's expectations. I look for what truly belongs to me, without deciding in advance what that is.

During practice, thoughts sometimes pass without catching hold of me. A clear calm settles, with an intense joy. It lasts a few seconds. Over the years, for me, those seconds have grown longer. I describe what I notice, without giving it a name.

If you want to see how it's worked at, the discovery workshop is made for that.

Where the method comes from

Gururaj refused all his life to have a cult made of him. He nonetheless taught until his death in 1988, and founded IFSU in 1975 so that the transmission would outlive him. In IFSU teaching, this instruction recurs: “Test it in the laboratory of your own life. If it benefits you, hold on to it. If not, leave it.” I pass it on in the same way: the techniques, I prescribe; what you find in them, each person observes for themselves.

Why it costs money

You don't pay for the techniques. They come from the IFSU lineage, a non-profit association, and I don't sell them. You pay for my time: the sessions, the one-to-one follow-up, the answers to your questions. That time is my work, which is why it has a price.

What can come up

It isn't always comfortable. Sometimes something unpleasant comes up during or after meditation: fear, anger, inner tension. The IFSU tradition describes this as a release of tension. We don't resist it and we don't judge ourselves; we come out of the meditation slowly. If someone is struggling a lot, we shorten the session to ten minutes. It's an adjustment, not a failure.

What I don't do

I don't belong to any Church and I don't ask you to believe in anything. I promise no result. If the practice brings up things that are outside my remit, I'll point you to a professional.
